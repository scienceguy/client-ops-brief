Growth doesn't fix a business that runs on memory

Four stories this week, one thread: the thing capping small consultancies is not demand. It is the undocumented layer between the sale and the delivery, where referrals, promises, fee language and capacity decisions all default to memory. Memory does not survive a busy quarter.

This week

Revenue doesn't fix three things

What happened. A consultant asked what actually changes at seven figures. The answer: confidence rises, and the roster gets more selective on its own. Three gaps stay exactly where they were: referrals running on memory, tool adoption that drops as you succeed, and the discipline to hold a fee once you have named it.

Why it matters. More revenue lets you absorb mess, which is why the mess survives. Each gap costs you in the currency you are trying to protect, which is delivery time. A referral engine held in one person's head goes quiet the month that person is buried in delivery, and a fee you did not hold turns into scope you did not price.

Do this. Open one file called referral-log.md and add a row for every client you won in the last twelve months: who sent them, what you did the week after the intro, and what you charged. Then paste your fee sentence verbatim into your proposal template so you stop improvising it under pressure.

The comfortable revenue trap

What happened. There is a point where you make enough to get comfortable but not enough to truly scale, and Cameron Hemphill has watched entrepreneurs get stuck there again and again. Instead of reinvesting to buy back their time, hire expertise and build infrastructure, they protect their current income.

Why it matters. Protecting income feels prudent. It is often the choice that caps you. At one to five people the trap looks like a calendar with no slack: every hour billable, nothing documented, no room to say yes to better work. The way out is not a hire you cannot afford. It is buying back two hours a week and spending them on the parts only you can build.

Do this. List every recurring task in your delivery and mark each one as judgment or presence. Take the presence task that repeats weekly and either write the checklist for it or remove it this week.

Pick ground the incumbents don't want

What happened. Tim Viands spent twenty-five years inside independent schools before founding IndySchool Consultancy. He entered an established market with no brand, competed against legacy firms by going where the incumbents were weakest, and built a business that grew year after year.

Why it matters. Positioning gets filed under marketing. For a small shop it is an operating decision, because the clients you accept decide how much custom work lands on your desk. A lane the big firms serve badly usually comes with shorter scopes and fewer approval layers, which is time returned to your week before you write a single process.

Do this. Write one sentence naming the segment where large firms are slowest and least interested. Then pull your last five proposals and count how many were actually for that segment.

Even the giants re-cut the book

What happened. Startup Fortune reports that Accenture is weighing a sale of its China consulting business at $1.5 billion. The report describes a consideration, not a completed transaction.

Why it matters. You will never run a book that size, but the logic holds at four clients: a client list is a set of positions, and concentration is a choice you remake every quarter. If one client pays most of your bills, a single procurement decision can rewrite your year. Those exits also move people and relationships around, and that is when small, well-placed shops get introduced.

Do this. Write down your three largest clients and each one's share of revenue. If one is over 40 percent, draft the first thirty days of a plan for losing them, then message one past referral source you have not spoken to this quarter.

Tool of the week

Obsidian

A folder of markdown files you own is the cheapest ops layer available: one note per client, one line per promise, nothing locked in a database you have to migrate out of later.

Use it: Make a folder called clients and one note per active client with four headings: Referred by, Promised, Fee language, Recurs weekly. Fill in Referred by for your last five clients before you close the laptop.

The pattern

Every item lands on the same point: the constraint is not what you can sell, it is what you never wrote down. Referrals, fee language, promises and capacity decisions all default to the founder's memory, and memory does not survive a busy quarter. The shops that clear the ceiling turn that layer into a file, a checklist or a workflow before they need it, not after.

One thing to try

None of this needs a new system. A file with four headings and twenty minutes on a Friday covers more ground than most software. Start with the client you would miss most if they left tomorrow.
